Read the complete synopsis

Up until the 1960s, elementary classroom instruction was based on the most effective procedures developed by classroom teachers, and American education was ranked number one in the world.

A downward trend began with "New Math" and reading programs that discouraged the use of phonics. These programs were developed within higher education and were supposed to be more effective than the procedures favored by the great majority of classroom teachers.

That downward trend accelerated in the 1980s, when the evaluation of classroom instruction was changed. Teachers were no longer recognized as having individual, yet effective, styles of teaching. Higher education created new criteria it considered more effective in the classroom, and this became the new basis for teacher evaluation and continued employment.

The author's related article, "Whatever Happened to Arithmetic," offers parents an explanation of the origins and use of "New Math" in the elementary school curriculum.

When the book was written, St. Lucie County, Florida had two K-8 schools. Today there are eleven, with a twelfth under construction. The middle school concept, with its varied mix of grade levels, was another product of the 1960s, and it apparently does not offer the educational continuity that a K-8 school does. Its sketchy record of success is most likely due to teacher competence, rather than the application of new psychological theory or school organization.

Meet the Author

Vincent B. Troiano

Retired Educator, Old Bridge Township School System, New Jersey

36 years teaching 27 years special education Grades 4–6 and special education

Vincent B. Troiano taught for the Old Bridge Township School System in New Jersey for 36 years: grades 4-6 for 9 years and special education for 27 years. His special education teaching career began in self-contained classes working with students identified as Emotionally Disturbed.

Over the course of his career, Troiano developed a firsthand understanding of the gap between educational policy and classroom reality — the gap at the heart of this book.

The Silent Voice in Education draws on decades of direct observation and experience to argue that teachers must have a meaningful voice in shaping education policy for the students they serve every day.
