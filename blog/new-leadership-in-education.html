Education Reform

How Education Reform Sidelined Classroom Teachers in the 1960s

By Vincent B. Troiano · August 29, 2026 · 3 min read

The 1950s and 1960s were a period of major change in American education. Mathematics reform accelerated through the New Math movement, while the modern middle school movement took shape in the 1960s. From my perspective as a classroom teacher, those changes also marked a shift in who had the strongest voice in shaping instruction.

The historical record supports the scale of the curricular change. ERIC’s history of New Math traces the reform movement from 1951 through the late 1970s, and scholarship on the middle school movement identifies 1963–1979 as its formative era. My argument is about what happened alongside those reforms: local systems increasingly looked to outside expertise and new administrative models, while the practical judgment of long-serving classroom teachers carried less weight.

Reading instruction also became a major arena of debate. Whatever one’s view of the programs adopted in a particular district, later evidence gives phonics a clear place in beginning reading: the National Reading Panel concluded that systematic phonics instruction significantly improves early reading outcomes and should be integrated into a complete reading program. That finding is a useful reminder that reforms should ultimately be judged by evidence and by what experienced teachers observe in real classrooms.

International comparisons also need more care than the familiar claim that the United States “used to be number one.” The 1964 First International Mathematics Study was among the earliest major cross-national comparisons, and it did not show the United States leading the participating systems. My concern is therefore not a lost numerical ranking; it is whether reform structures made it easier or harder for classroom experience to influence decisions.
