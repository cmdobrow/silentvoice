New Math vs. Arithmetic: Why Math Facts Matter for Algebra

By Vincent B. Troiano · August 2026 · 4 min read

The 2008 National Mathematics Advisory Panel did not use the phrase “lack of ease of operations.” What it did say was more concrete: preparation for algebra requires conceptual understanding, computational fluency, and fast access to number combinations.

The Panel’s final report says that computational proficiency with whole-number operations depends on practice that develops automatic recall of addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division facts, together with fluency in standard algorithms. It also says these procedural skills and conceptual understanding are mutually supportive rather than competing approaches.

Why math facts still matter

That finding puts a research-backed foundation under something many experienced elementary teachers have long believed: students benefit when basic facts become quick and reliable enough that they do not consume all of a learner’s attention. The Panel identified fluency with whole numbers and fractions as part of the critical foundation for algebra, while also emphasizing conceptual understanding and problem solving.

"The inclusion of multiplication facts in elementary math programs, by classroom teachers, to supplement 'new math,' is a true expression of the silent voice in education."

Teachers filled the gap themselves

The inclusion of multiplication facts in elementary math programs, added quietly by classroom teachers to supplement new math, is a genuine expression of the silent voice in education. I did it myself, back when the practice was actively discouraged in new math programs during the 1960s. And teachers were still doing the same thing in 2018, in a new math program where it remained officially forbidden.

A pattern that never really stopped

From the 1960s to 2018, the same quiet workaround shows up again and again: teachers slipping number facts back into instruction, regardless of what the official program allowed. That consistency, across sixty years, is itself evidence of where the real expertise in the classroom has always lived.

Knowing the tables is not the same as knowing arithmetic

Simply knowing that multiplication tables exist gives elementary math students some assistance, but it does not offer the same confidence and competence that a full traditional arithmetic program builds. It was that deeper efficiency, not just the tables themselves, that most likely gave students the confidence to move on to algebra, back before new math was introduced into school systems across the United States.

The 2008 report is therefore more useful when read on its own terms. It does not frame the issue as “old math” versus “new math”; it calls for a combination of conceptual understanding, procedural fluency, problem solving, and automatic recall of basic facts. My argument is that arithmetic practice belongs squarely inside that combination.
